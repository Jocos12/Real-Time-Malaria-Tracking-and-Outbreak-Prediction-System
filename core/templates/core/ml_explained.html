How the Machine Learning Works
A plain-language explanation of the two PySpark MLlib models powering this dashboard

Model 1 — Individual Case Risk Random Forest

Goal: for a single reported patient, predict whether their test is likely to come back positive.

1

What data goes in (features)

For each case, the model looks at the patient's own data plus the local weather at the time it was reported:

Patient age Region Gender Body temperature Ambient temperature Humidity Rainfall (mm) Number of symptoms
2

How the algorithm decides (Random Forest)

A Random Forest builds 100 decision trees, each one asking a series of yes/no questions like "is rainfall above X mm?" or "is age below Y?". Each tree votes "positive" or "negative", and the forest takes the majority vote. Using many trees instead of one avoids relying on a single, possibly wrong, rule.

3

What comes out (prediction)

For every case, the model outputs a 0 (likely negative) or 1 (likely positive), plus a probability score. This is stored and shown on the Analytics page as a "risk score" per region.

4

How we know if it's any good (evaluation)

Before trusting the model, we split the data: 80% to train it, 20% kept aside to test it on cases it has never seen. We then measure:

AUC
How well the model separates positive from negative cases overall. 0.5 = random guessing, 1.0 = perfect.
Accuracy
Percentage of cases the model got right, positive or negative.
Precision
Of the cases predicted positive, how many actually were. High precision = few false alarms.
Recall
Of the cases that were actually positive, how many the model caught. High recall = few missed cases.

Your exact scores from training are printed in ml_case_log.txt — copy them into your report.

5

How it is deployed

The trained model is saved to HDFS at /malaria/models/case_risk_rf. A batch scoring script then applies it to every case and writes the predictions to /malaria/predictions/case_risk, which this dashboard reads to build the "Average Risk Score by Region" table.

Model 2 — Regional Outbreak Forecast Gradient Boosted Trees

Goal: for a given region and day, predict whether it is heading into an outbreak (an unusually high number of positive cases).

1

Preparing the data (aggregation)

Individual cases are grouped by region and date. For each region/day, we compute: total cases, positive cases, and the average rainfall, humidity, temperature and symptom count that day. This turns thousands of individual reports into one row per region per day.

2

Defining "outbreak" (the label)

We calculate the 75th percentile of daily positive cases across all regions — meaning the busiest 25% of region-days. Any day above that threshold is labeled an outbreak (1), otherwise stable (0). This threshold adapts automatically to your data volume instead of using a fixed number.

3

How the algorithm decides (Gradient Boosted Trees)

GBT builds trees one after another, where each new tree focuses on fixing the mistakes of the previous ones. After 50 rounds, the combined trees form a strong predictor of outbreak risk from total cases, rainfall, humidity, temperature and symptom levels.

Region Total cases that day Avg rainfall Avg humidity Avg temperature Avg symptom count
4

Evaluation

Same idea as Model 1: 80/20 train-test split, then AUC, F1-score, precision and recall are calculated. See ml_outbreak_log.txt for your exact numbers.

5

How it is deployed

The model is saved to /malaria/models/outbreak_gbt. A batch job scores every region/day and writes results to /malaria/predictions/outbreak_risk — this feeds the red "Outbreak Alert" banner and the prediction table on the Analytics page.

Why two models instead of one?

Model 1 works at the individual level — useful for triage and understanding which patient traits correlate with malaria. Model 2 works at the regional/daily level — useful for public health decisions like "where should we send mosquito nets this week?" Together they cover both the Tracking and the Outbreak Prediction parts of this project.
